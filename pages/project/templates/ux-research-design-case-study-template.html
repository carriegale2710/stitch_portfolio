01 / research plan

What I needed to learn

The original concept assumed people wanted a more complete task manager. Discovery focused instead on the point where users decided whether a planning tool felt helpful or overwhelming.

participant criteria

[Who I spoke to]

People who used a digital task tool at least weekly but had abandoned or ignored it in the previous month.

6 interviews
research method

[How I learned]

design principle
Reduce visible choice at planning time, but do not hide the user’s larger backlog.
Synthesis from research
research synthesis → product decision

I removed the traditional all-tasks-first dashboard. The first screen asks users to choose up to three intentional tasks, while the full backlog remains one click away for reassurance rather than becoming the default cognitive load.

main design tension

Reduce overwhelm without creating an oversimplified toy. The solution needed to protect focus while preserving access to planning detail when a user actively sought it.

04 / iteration

Design iterations

what changed

Progressive disclosure

The backlog moved behind an explicit “view all tasks” action.

why

Less decision friction

The primary screen presents only information needed for today’s choice.

trade-off

Less immediate control

Power users need one additional interaction to access everything.

06 / reflection

What I learned

Research changed the scope

The problem was prioritisation anxiety, not missing task-management features.

Evidence beats preference

I kept the backlog feature because participants needed reassurance, even though minimalism suggested removing it.

Next validation step

Test retention over two weeks; one successful session does not prove a daily habit.
